How Common is Breast Cancer?
Breast cancer is incredibly common. In the United States, about 1 in 8 women (approximately 13%) will develop breast cancer in their lifetime. It is the second leading cause of cancer death among U.S. women, behind only lung cancer.
This year alone, over 380,000 women in the U.S. will be diagnosed with some form of breast cancer. While it predominantly affects women, men can also develop breast cancer, though it is much rarer.

How Is Breast Cancer Detected?
Finding breast cancer early is critical. It is typically identified through a combination of imaging and tissue sampling:
Screening Mammograms: Low-dose X-rays of the breast that can often detect cancer before symptoms appear.
Diagnostic Imaging: If a lump is felt or a screening mammogram shows an abnormality, doctors will use diagnostic mammograms, ultrasounds, or MRIs to get a clearer picture.
Biopsy: The only definitive way to diagnose breast cancer is by removing a small sample of tissue (often using a hollow needle) and examining it under a microscope.

What Increases the Risk?
There are many possible risk factors for breast cancer. Some cannot be changed, while others are related to lifestyle.
Important risk factors include:
Getting older: Most breast cancers are diagnosed after age 50.
Family history: Having a close relative with breast cancer can increase risk.
Inherited gene mutations: Changes in genes such as BRCA1 and BRCA2 can significantly increase the risk.
Dense breasts: Dense breast tissue can increase risk and can also make tumors harder to see on a mammogram.
Previous breast cancer: Having breast cancer in the past increases the chance of developing it again.
Previous radiation therapy: Radiation to the chest at a young age can increase risk.
Alcohol use: Breast cancer risk increases as alcohol consumption increases.
Physical inactivity and obesity after menopause: These can also increase risk.

Staging and Biological Subtypes?
Breast cancer is evaluated using the TNM System, but today's staging is highly dependent on the cancer's biology, not just its size.
The TNM Basics:
T (Tumor): The size of the original tumor.
N (Node): Whether cancer has spread to the lymph nodes (often in the underarm).
M (Metastasis): Whether it has spread to distant organs like the bones, liver, lungs, or brain.

Stage 0: Abnormal/cancerous cells are still confined to where they started, such as ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS).
Stage I: A relatively small cancer that has not spread significantly.
Stage II: The cancer is larger and/or has spread to some nearby lymph nodes.
Stage III: The cancer has spread more extensively to nearby tissues or lymph nodes.
Stage IV: The cancer has spread to distant parts of the body and is called metastatic breast cancer.

The Subtypes (Receptor Status):
When doctors biopsy a tumor, they test it for three key proteins. These results can actually "down-stage" or "up-stage" the cancer because they predict how aggressive it is and how well it will respond to targeted therapies:
Estrogen Receptor (ER) / Progesterone Receptor (PR): Cancers that use these hormones to grow are called Hormone Receptor-Positive (HR+).
HER2 Status: Cancers with high levels of the HER2 protein grow rapidly but respond well to specialized drugs.
Triple-Negative: Cancers that test negative for all three receptors. This type tends to grow faster and has fewer targeted treatment options.

Current Available Treatments?
Treatments are planned by a multidisciplinary team based on the specific subtype and stage:
Surgery: The most common first step. Options range from a lumpectomy (removing just the tumor and a margin of healthy tissue) to a mastectomy (removing the entire breast).
Radiation Therapy: High-energy rays used to destroy microscopic cancer cells left behind after surgery.
Chemotherapy: Often used before surgery (neoadjuvant) to shrink large tumors, or after (adjuvant) to kill circulating cancer cells.
Hormone (Endocrine) Therapy: Daily medications taken for 5 to 10 years to block hormones from fueling HR+ cancers.
Targeted Therapy: Drugs designed specifically to attack the HER2 protein in HER2-positive cancers.

Life Expectancy and Survival Rates?
Because of vast improvements in screening and treatment, the overall 5-year relative survival rate for breast cancer in the U.S. is very high at 91% to 92%.
